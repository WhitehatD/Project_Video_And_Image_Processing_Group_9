\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Overall Approach}

Our solution uses transfer learning with a pretrained convolutional neural network, combined with an ensemble of independently trained models and test-time augmentation (TTA). Prior work has shown that models pretrained on large-scale image datasets carry low-level feature detectors (edges, curves, textures) that transfer well to handwritten character recognition, even when the source and target domains differ \citep{krizhevsky2012imagenet, deng2009imagenet}.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We use EfficientNet-B0 \citep{tan2019efficientnet} as the backbone. EfficientNet-B0 applies compound scaling, which jointly scales network depth, width, and resolution by a fixed ratio. This gives a strong accuracy-to-parameter ratio compared to manually designed architectures. The backbone is initialised with ImageNet-pretrained weights \citep{deng2009imagenet}.

The original classifier head is replaced with a dropout layer ($p = 0.3$) \citep{srivastava2014dropout} followed by a linear layer mapping from 1280 features to 10 class logits. All backbone layers are fine-tuned end-to-end throughout training.

\subsection{Input Preprocessing}

The dataset contains $32 \times 32$ grayscale images. Since the pretrained backbone expects three-channel input, we replicate the grayscale channel across all three colour channels. Images are resized to $96 \times 96$ pixels with bilinear interpolation and normalised using the ImageNet channel statistics: mean $\mu = (0.485, 0.456, 0.406)$ and standard deviation $\sigma = (0.229, 0.224, 0.225)$.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}

The following augmentations are applied on-the-fly during training:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random rotation:} each image is rotated by a random angle drawn uniformly from $[-15^\circ, +15^\circ]$.
    \item \textbf{Random affine:} random translation of up to 10\% of the image size and random scaling in $[0.9, 1.1]$.
    \item \textbf{Random perspective:} projective distortion with a distortion scale of 0.2 and application probability 0.5.
    \item \textbf{Random erasing:} a randomly positioned rectangular region is replaced with noise with probability 0.1 \citep{zhong2020random}.
\end{itemize}

Horizontal flipping is not applied. The handwritten symbols in this dataset are not left-right symmetric, so flipping would alter the visual identity of several characters and effectively corrupt the class labels.

\subsection{Regularisation}

\paragraph{Mixup.} At each training step, pairs of samples $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ and $(\mathbf{x}_j, y_j)$ are combined as:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}} &= \lambda \mathbf{x}_i + (1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_j, \\
    \tilde{y}          &= \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j,
\end{align}
where $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ with $\alpha = 0.3$ \citep{zhang2018mixup}. This pushes the model to predict smoothly between training examples rather than relying on sharp decision boundaries.

\paragraph{Label smoothing.} Rather than training on one-hot targets, we use label smoothing with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ \citep{muller2019label}. The smoothed target for class $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{p}(k) = (1 - \varepsilon)\,\mathbf{1}[k = y] + \frac{\varepsilon}{K},
\end{equation}
where $K = 10$. This stops the model from assigning arbitrarily high confidence to training examples and generally improves calibration.

\subsection{Optimisation}

We use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} as the optimiser. Unlike standard Adam, AdamW applies weight decay directly to the parameters rather than folding it into the gradient update, which tends to generalise better. The learning rate follows a cosine annealing schedule \citep{loshchilov2017cosine} with a linear warmup over the first three epochs. The peak learning rate is $5 \times 10^{-4}$, decaying to near zero over 40 epochs.

Training uses automatic mixed precision (AMP) \citep{micikevicius2018mixed}, running forward and backward passes in float16 with a GradScaler to keep the training numerically stable. Gradient norms are clipped to 1.0.

\subsection{Multi-Seed Ensemble}

Three models are trained independently using random seeds 42, 123, and 456. Each seed determines weight initialisation, batch order, and stochastic augmentation, so the models learn somewhat different decision boundaries. At inference time, the softmax probability vectors from all three models are averaged:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}(k) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} p_m(k), \quad M = 3,
\end{equation}
and the final prediction is $\hat{y} = \arg\max_k \hat{p}(k)$. Averaging over multiple seeds reduces prediction variance and makes the ensemble more robust to individual model errors.

\subsection{Test-Time Augmentation}

At inference time, five forward passes are run per test image: one on the original, and four on rotated copies at $+8^\circ$, $-8^\circ$, $+15^\circ$, and $-15^\circ$. The resulting softmax distributions are averaged before being combined with the ensemble. Each test image is ultimately processed by $3~\text{seeds} \times 5~\text{TTA passes} = 15$ forward passes in total, and the class with the highest mean probability is taken as the final prediction.
